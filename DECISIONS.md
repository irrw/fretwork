# Fretwork — Decision History

A record of what got built, why, and the priorities driving it. Written for future-us (or anyone forking this) to understand intent, not just implementation.

## Core intent

Fretwork is a mobile-first scale/fingering visualizer for fretted instruments. Two priorities have overridden almost every other design decision since day one:

1. **Maximum screen real estate for the fretboard itself.** The diagram is the product; everything else — instrument, tuning, root, scale, labels — is secondary chrome that should take up as little permanent space as possible.
2. **Minimal, progressively-revealed UI.** Controls stay hidden until tapped rather than sitting visible at all times. Several rounds of this app's history are literally "remove the header," "collapse this into fewer taps," "stop this from needing to scroll."

Non-goal, stated explicitly: this is not trying to out-feature the existing market of scale/fretboard apps, which is already saturated. Value is for the primary user and a small circle of similarly-minded players (see Distribution below), not for maximizing install numbers.

## Product decisions, in rough order

- **Orientation**: fretboard renders as the player's own top-down view (looking down at your own neck while playing) — nut at top, frets increasing downward, vertical scroll — not the mirrored "sitting across from the guitarist" view common in chord-chart apps. Strings run low-to-high left-to-right.
- **Portrait-only**: the installed app is locked to portrait (`orientation: 'portrait'` in the PWA manifest, `vite.config.js`). The fretboard is a tall vertical neck, and portrait gives it the most screen, in line with the real-estate priority above. Landscape isn't a supported layout. The lock only takes effect where the platform honors it: installed apps on Android (Chrome, Vivaldi) stay portrait, but regular browser tabs always rotate, and iOS (Safari, home-screen apps) doesn't appear to honor the manifest's orientation at all, with no other web-side way to lock it. Rotation there is tolerated, not designed for.
- **Note tokens**: filled circle = root (accent color), outlined circle filled with page background = other in-scale note, nothing rendered = not in scale. Open strings not in the scale still show a bare letter (no ring) so the string's identity is never fully hidden.
- **Theme**: dark (ebony/brass, evoking the instrument's own wood and hardware) is default, with a light "parchment" theme as an explicit toggle, not a system-preference auto-switch.
- **Box positions**: went through several iterations before landing on the current model.
  - First attempt: algorithmically *suggested* box positions by clustering the frets where the root note lands across all strings (root-note density as a proxy for "natural hand position").
  - Rejected in favor of full manual control: **two-tap selection** — first tap drops an anchor, every other fret number becomes an obvious white "pick width" button, second tap sets the box's actual width. No fixed span. This gives the user full control over playable box shapes (matters for scales like harmonic/melodic minor with an augmented-2nd interval that a fixed 4-fret box can clip).
  - Outside the active box, finger-position mode shows "–" ("unknown") rather than guessing — fingering numbers are meaningless without a chosen hand position. (An earlier version fell back to scale degrees there; the dash replaced it along with the per-instrument fingering rules.)
- **Double-stop overlay**: diatonic 3rds/6ths only, computed live from the interval data (not hardcoded per scale), scoped to the active box only to avoid neck-wide visual noise. Built after "how do we surface double stops" was explicitly asked as an open design question and three concrete directions were compared before picking this one.
- **Customizable scale list**: default visible set is curated (not all 12 modes/scales shown at once); anything else is reachable via an explicit edit mode (pencil icon → hide/restore chips) plus a "preview chip" affordance — 1–2 hidden scales always peek into the visible row so the existence of more options is discoverable without a manual.
- **Label-mode toggle**: originally a single text chip ("Notes"/"Degrees"/"Fingers"). Identified as a discoverability failure — the word had no visual relationship to what's actually drawn on the neck, and "Degrees" vs. "Fingers" were both just numbers with no way to tell them apart at a glance. Replaced with a three-dot mini legend: a literal note-letter token, a degree token shown *with an accidental* (so it can't be confused with a bare finger number), and a fingerprint icon for finger mode. Tapping any dot jumps straight to that mode.
- **Bottom bar consolidation**: repeatedly compressed as the control count grew. Root+Scale merged into one "Key" chip; Instrument+Tuning+Theme+Double-Stops merged into a single overflow ("•••") sheet — reasoning being that instrument/tuning is a *session-level* choice a player sets once and rarely touches, while root/scale changes constantly during practice. The two shouldn't compete for the same row.
- **Mandolin as default instrument** (pre-repo prototyping): the project's actual primary user plays mandolin/bluegrass, not guitar. Guitar was the original scaffolding default from early prototyping; mandolin is now what loads first for all users. (See **First-run instrument picker** under Feature ideas for replacing this assumption with an explicit choice.)
- **Dorian restored to default visible scales** (pre-repo prototyping): initially cut for being "impractical" for straight bluegrass repertoire, but old-time/modal fiddle tunes (e.g. "Shady Grove") do lean Dorian/Mixolydian, so it's back in the curated default set rather than requiring a manual restore.
- **File consolidation**: the project briefly forked into `_stable` / `_v2` / `_boxes_experiment` variants during exploration. Confirmed via diff that the experiment branch was a strict superset and collapsed everything back to one canonical `fretwork.jsx`. Lesson carried forward: prefer git branches over filename-suffix forks once this is a real repo — branches are the tool this pattern was standing in for.
- **Mandolin's fifths tuning**: worth noting for later — mandolin's uniform perfect-fifths tuning (G-D-A-E) makes adjacent-string interval shapes identical across every string pair, unlike guitar's mixed fourths-plus-a-third tuning. That symmetry isn't exploited anywhere in the code today (the box-suggestion heuristic that would have used it was replaced by manual selection), but if algorithmic box-position suggestions ever come back, mandolin is the instrument where a single computed shape could be mechanically transposed across strings instead of re-derived per pair.

## Persistence

Settings (instrument, tuning, root, scale, label mode, theme, hidden-scale list, double-stop mode, and the onboarding tooltip's don't-show-again flag) persist via `localStorage` under a single versioned key (`fretwork.prefs.v1`). All reads/writes are try/catch-wrapped and fail silently — `localStorage` is unavailable inside the Claude.ai artifact sandbox this was prototyped in, so persistence simply no-ops there; it activates for real once deployed to actual static hosting.

## Distribution & business context

- **No commercial ambition.** The scale/fretboard-visualizer space is already saturated; there isn't a market gap worth chasing here. Value is personal-plus-community, not growth.
- **Open source, static hosting.** No backend, no database — a static build costs nothing to host at this project's realistic traffic (a few hundred KB per load; even the most conservative free tier's bandwidth cap implies well over 100,000 loads/month before it'd matter).
- **Host decision (final): Vercel.** Chosen over GitHub Pages / Cloudflare Pages because its GitHub integration gives per-PR preview deploys out of the box — no custom workflow needed for that, and it was previously listed below as explicitly deferred. Free Hobby tier fits this project's no-commercial-ambition, low-traffic profile.
- **Distribution model: word of mouth within the actual community**, not app-store/SEO-driven growth — mandolin/bluegrass forums, subreddits, in-person jams. Matches the scale of the intended audience; general-audience discovery mechanisms would be solving a problem this project doesn't have.
- **PWA install path** ("Add to Home Screen") is the answer to cross-platform friction (iOS sideloading is effectively blocked; Android APK distribution isn't universal) — installable from the same static site with no app-store review process on either platform.

## CI/CD plan

- **Claude.ai chat** (this environment) has no persistent storage and no network egress — it can produce files but cannot push them anywhere. It's the design/iteration surface, not the shipping mechanism.
- **Claude Code** (CLI/desktop, run locally with real filesystem + git/gh credentials) is the tool that actually commits, pushes, and opens PRs. The intended loop: iterate here → hand finished code to Claude Code → it pushes to a branch → PR → merge.
- **Repo shape**: single repo, `main` = production. Feature branches replace the old filename-suffix forking pattern (see File consolidation above).
- **Deploy pipeline: Vercel**, imported directly from the GitHub repo (once connected: pushes to `main` deploy to production, every PR gets its own preview URL automatically). No custom GitHub Actions workflow needed for this — Vercel's GitHub integration handles build + deploy on its own.
- **CI pipeline: GitHub Actions** (`.github/workflows/ci.yml`), triggered `on: pull_request` — runs `npm ci && npm run build` as an independent build check, separate from Vercel's own build.
- **No longer deferred**: per-branch preview URLs — solved by moving to Vercel (see Distribution & business context above).

## Follow-ups / known gaps

- **Desktop fretboard sizing (fixed, pre-repo)**: the fretboard was pixel-sized for mobile and looked tiny on tall desktop viewports. It now scales up (via a `ResizeObserver`-driven CSS transform, floored at 1x) to fill available vertical space; mobile layout is unaffected since the scale never drops below 1.
- **Per-instrument inlay dots (fixed, #15)**: every instrument used to share guitar's position markers (3, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15). Mandolin and ukulele mark the 10th fret rather than the 9th, so a player navigating by the dots was one fret off. Inlays now live on each `INSTRUMENTS` entry: guitar 3/5/7/9/12/15, mandolin 3/5/7/10/12/15 (the 3rd-fret dot is common on mandolin but not universal — kept by choice), ukulele 5/7/10/12/15. The 12th fret keeps the double dot on all of them. A future banjo entry would use 3/5/7/10/12/15.
- **Desktop bottom bar is under-designed (open)**: the bottom control bar (Key chip, label-mode legend, ••• overflow sheet) was built entirely around mobile tap targets and thumb reach. On desktop it still works but doesn't take advantage of the extra space, pointer precision, or hover affordances — e.g. the overflow sheet exists mainly to save space that desktop actually has, and there's no keyboard/hover interaction model. Two concrete sub-issues (the second is already resolved):
  1. **Width/position on desktop.** The bar spans full viewport width and stays pinned to the bottom at every breakpoint, unchanged from mobile. On a wide window this strands related controls at opposite edges (Key chip far left, ••• far right) with a large dead gap between them. Worth first asking whether a bottom-pinned bar is the right pattern for desktop at all — vs. e.g. a compact top toolbar — before just capping its width and centering it.
  2. **Panel z-axis (done).** Opening the Key or ••• panel used to shrink the fretboard's `flex: 1` scroll area, re-triggering the `fitScale` recompute and reflowing the whole neck. The panel is now absolutely positioned above the bottom bar, overlaying the fretboard, with the existing backdrop-dismiss behavior, so opening it no longer changes the scroll area's size (see the comment on the bottom-bar wrapper in `src/fretwork.jsx`). This was already in place in the initial commit; this note was just never updated.
- **iOS home-screen app: light background flashed behind the app on rotation (fixed, #17)**: reported on an iPhone with the app installed from Safari (Add to Home Screen). With the in-app theme set to dark, every rotation briefly showed an off-white background behind the app while its content rotated; on first launch after install there was also a persistent light-colored gap that went away after the first rotation.

  Cause: the page behind the app (the `html`/`body`/`#root` background in `index.html`) followed the **OS** light/dark setting via `@media (prefers-color-scheme: light)` (added in #12), while the app itself follows the **in-app** theme toggle, which defaults to dark regardless of the OS. On a light-mode phone the two disagreed, and whatever exposed the page behind the app (iOS repainting during rotation before the `100dvh` container re-laid out, and probably a first-launch viewport-height miscalculation in iOS standalone mode) showed the wrong color.

  Fix: the page background now follows the in-app theme. A small inline script in `index.html` reads the saved theme from `fretwork.prefs.v1` before first paint and sets `data-theme` on `<html>` (defaulting to dark, the app's own default), CSS keys the background off that attribute, and a `useEffect` in `src/fretwork.jsx` keeps it in sync when the theme is toggled. The OS-scheme media query is gone: it contradicted the explicit-toggle theme decision above, and also briefly flashed cream to new users on light-mode phones before the app painted dark. This removes the color mismatch; any iOS re-layout exposure is now the same color as the app. If a first-launch gap is a real iOS viewport bug it may still exist, just invisible — re-check on the device.

- **Tooltip: close the two-tap loop (fixed, #18)**: the onboarding tooltip used to explain only step 1 ("tap a fret to start a box") and disappeared on the first fret tap, before the user had necessarily learned step 2. It's now chained: the first tap hides the step-1 tooltip and shows a one-time step-2 tooltip ("Now tap another fret to set the box's width") pointing two frets past the anchor, or two before it near the end of the neck so it stays on the board. Step 2 goes away once a box width is set or the anchor is cancelled, and doesn't come back for later boxes in the same session. Both steps share one dismissal and one persisted don't-show-again flag (`dontShowFretTip`), so the sequence is a single onboarding, not two separate nags. The tooltip markup is shared via an `OnboardingTip` component in `src/fretwork.jsx`.
- **Neck capped at 15 frets, real necks go further (open)**: every entry in `INSTRUMENTS` (`src/fretwork.jsx`) hardcodes `frets: 15`, but real guitars and mandolins commonly extend well past that (guitars typically 19-24 frets, mandolins 20-24, most 5-string banjos 22); ukulele's realistic range hasn't been checked. Free-scrolling the neck to reach those extra frets was tried before and rejected — it rendered glitchily across different viewport sizes and made the UI feel fidgety (related to the `fitScale`/`ResizeObserver` sizing work above). One direction floated but not designed: paginate the neck — a "see further down the neck" affordance for advanced players, rather than turning it into a free-scrolling surface. Needs real per-instrument fret-count research plus a concrete pagination UX before implementing (same "research before building" lesson as the finger-position work below).
- **5-string banjo (added, #19)**: banjo is now a fourth instrument, as a 5-string with tunings Open G (gDGBD), Double C (gCGCD), Sawmill (gDGCD), Open D (f#DF#AD) and Drop C (gCGBD). Decisions made along the way:
  - **Short 5th string.** The data model's "every string spans the nut to the last fret" assumption is relaxed: a tuning's string can be a plain note name or `{ note, startFret }`. The 5th string is `{ startFret: 5 }`: no open note at the nut, its line starts at the 5th fret, its open note shows in the fret-5 row (bare letter if not in the scale, "O" in finger mode, the same as a nut open string), with a small nut drawn just below it, and notes above that are counted from its own open note.
  - **String order.** The 5th string is leftmost even though it's pitched high, because physically it sits on the bass side; this keeps the player's own top-down view consistent with the other instruments.
  - **Double stops skip the 5th string.** It's a drone, not a melodic double-stop partner.
  - **Inlays** are 3/5/7/10/12/15, and are centered on the four full-length strings rather than the whole board, so they don't hide under the middle string's notes. Instruments without a short string are unchanged.
  - **Finger position mode shows "–" for banjo**: banjo fingering conventions haven't been researched (see the finger-position follow-up below), so it doesn't borrow another instrument's rule.
  - **Deferred:** 4-string tenor (CGDA) and plectrum (CGBD) banjo (pure data additions if wanted); the 22-fret neck most 5-string banjos have (blocked on the neck-length follow-up above); and capo/railroad-spike handling for the 5th string.
- **Finger-position fingering conventions need research before building on them (open)**: an earlier attempt at a "Finger position" label mode encoded fingering rules from a single description too literally and produced wrong answers for real players (see git history on the label-mode menu work). Research into published mandolin/fiddle and guitar pedagogy found the two instrument families use genuinely different conventions — guitar/ukulele use a fixed one-finger-per-fret position (CAGED-style) independent of which frets are diatonic, while mandolin follows a sequential "next scale note, next finger" rule per string, skipping non-scale frets entirely. The current implementation (gated behind an EXPERIMENTAL toggle in the ellipsis menu, not the primary Labels menu) reflects that research and has been checked against real playing, but treat any further fingering claims — especially for banjo, which now exists but shows "–" in finger mode until its conventions are researched — as needing the same research-first treatment before shipping them as authoritative.
- **Key/scale note-equivalence surfacing (open)**: Explored ways to let the user know when two (root, scale) selections produce identical notes minus the root (e.g. relative major/minor). Detection is cheap and generic — computed from the existing `SCALES` interval data ([src/fretwork.jsx](src/fretwork.jsx)) rather than hardcoded per pair: two selections are equivalent iff their pitch-class sets match. Applied to the current scale list, this covers more than just relative minor — all seven diatonic modes (Major/Dorian/Phrygian/Lydian/Mixolydian/Minor/Locrian) form one family sharing a single note collection, Major and Minor Pentatonic form a pair, and Blues, Harmonic Minor, and Melodic Minor each currently have no equivalent partner.

  Several UI directions were compared (passive caption under the Key chip, badges on scale pills, a one-tap "reroot" swap, long-press-to-preview on the fretboard, reframing the picker around collection-then-tonic) before landing on the most promising one to prototype: a circle-of-fifths-style dial, radio-tuner metaphor — fixed window, rotating collection underneath, with a secondary control to re-root within the collection without changing the notes. Built and compared two versions of this side by side (a cheap scroll-snap linear reel vs. a literal rotating dial with drag/angle geometry) in a working prototype, since circular drag was flagged as only worth the added complexity if it earned its keep.

  Not yet decided: fifths order vs. chromatic order for the outer collection control (fifths matches key-signature theory; chromatic matches the fretboard's own layout); whether the reel's simpler interaction holds up against the dial once both have been used for a while; whether this replaces the existing Key chip outright or ships as an opt-in "explore" mode alongside the curated default, consistent with how the scale-pill list already hides complexity behind edit mode.

- **Root token readability (bold done in #23; light-mode color open)**: root labels were hard to read against the brass fill in both themes, so they're now bold (IBM Plex Mono 700, added to the font request so it isn't synthesized). Measured contrast: dark theme's dark-on-brass is 7.3:1 (fine; the thin weight at 11px was the problem), but light theme's cream-on-brass (`#F1E9D8` on `#B4761F`) is only 3.1:1, under the usual 4.5:1 for text. Switching light's `rootText` to the dark text color (`#2A2318`) would raise it to 4.1:1, still short; reaching 4.5:1 would also mean darkening the light theme's brass. Revisit after seeing bold on a phone.
- **Window Controls Overlay for desktop installs (deferred)**: Chrome's PWA audit suggests setting `display_override: ["window-controls-overlay", ...]` in the manifest so an installed desktop PWA can draw its own title bar instead of the OS default. Deferred rather than done because the app has no header today — the fretboard starts immediately below `env(safe-area-inset-top)` ([src/fretwork.jsx](src/fretwork.jsx:302)), and all controls live in the bottom bar (see Bottom bar consolidation above). Turning on WCO without reserving space via `env(titlebar-area-x/y/width/height)` would let the OS window buttons overlap the fret-number gutter/nut on Chromium desktop installs — worse than not having the feature. Desktop-only, Chromium-only benefit, so not worth the header-design work unless desktop installs become a real usage pattern; revisit alongside the "Desktop bottom bar is under-designed" follow-up above if that happens, since both would touch the same territory (adding real desktop-specific chrome).

## Feature ideas (not yet designed)

Captured so they don't get lost. None of these are committed to; each needs a design pass before building.

- **Interval-relationship coloring (idea)**: color note tokens by their relationship to the root. Two possible shapes: (a) one color per interval, or (b) selectable *groups* of relationships — e.g. "show the 1-4-5," "show the minor intervals (b3, b6, b7)." Working hunch: coloring all 12 intervals at once will be overwhelming and less useful than no color at all, so (b) is the more promising direction — color is a spotlight on one group at a time, everything else stays neutral. Open questions: which groups to offer by default; what the palette is (must read in both the ebony and parchment themes, and must not collide with the root's brass accent or the double-stop connectors); how it interacts with the existing label modes and the active box.
- **Highlight by string (built, #20)**: tapping a string's cell in the open-string row toggles it; several strings can be selected. Selected strings get the same highlight band as the fret box: down the whole neck with no box, or only where they intersect the box (e.g. frets 5–9 × strings A and E → just that rectangle). The open-string cell stays tinted so the selection is visible either way. Clearing the box falls back to the full-neck columns; clearing all strings falls back to today's full-width box. Double stops follow the same focus area: only between adjacent *selected* strings, within the box rows if there is one, or along the whole neck if not (with no box and no strings, still none). Resolved open questions:
  - **Tap target**: the open-string cells weren't interactive before, so there's nothing to confuse a string tap with. The banjo's 5th string has no open note at the nut; its empty cell at the top of the column is the toggle.
  - **Persistence**: the selection survives key/scale changes like the box does, clears on instrument change (string count changes), and isn't saved across reloads (the box isn't either).
  - **Dimming outside the focus area (added, #23)**: the band alone read too subtly for a small intersection, so notes outside the focus area now fade to 30%, the same whether the focus comes from a fret box, selected strings, or both. (A lighter 50% for a box alone was tried and dropped: one level is simpler and reads consistently.) Open-string notes double as the string toggles, so they stay at full strength with a box alone, and fade only to 60% when other strings are selected. Both ends of a drawn double stop stay at full strength even if the partner note sits just outside the box (the 3rd/6th search reaches up to 3 frets past it). Faded tokens sit on an opaque backing in the page color, so the string line doesn't show through them. Fades animate over 150 ms. Nothing changes until a box or string is selected.
  - **Band strength (#21):** the dark theme's band was raised from 10% to 20% brass after it read too faintly on a real phone (light mode's 12% was fine). By measured lightness the 10% dark band was already as strong as light's, but on near-black screens the tint carries almost no visible hue and phones flatten near-black shades, so dark needs a stronger value than the numbers suggest. 16% and 24% were also compared; 24% started to look like a brown panel and dulled the root's brass.
- **Label double-stop connectors with their chord function (idea, needs the theory settled first)**: see the note below — a 3rd/6th dyad is two notes of a triad, so each connector could carry a Roman-numeral label telling the player what chord that double stop implies ("this is a V-chord double stop").

- **Custom tunings (idea)**: a **(+)** button at the end of the tuning chips in the ••• sheet adds a new chip with a text field. The user types the open-string notes low → high, one letter per string (e.g. `DADGAD` replaces `EADGBE`). Input is forced to uppercase and capped at the current instrument's string count (6 for guitar, 5 for banjo, 4 for mandolin and ukulele); a tuning only saves once every string has a note. This fits the existing data model: each tuning is already just an array of note names low → high (`INSTRUMENTS[...].tunings` in `src/fretwork.jsx`), with no octave information, so letters are enough to describe a tuning. Custom tunings would be stored per instrument in the existing `fretwork.prefs.v1` localStorage prefs.

  Things to resolve before building:
  - **Sharps and flats.** Letters alone can't express a sharp or flat string. The built-in Open D is `D A D F# A D`, and down-tuned guitars (E♭ standard) are common. Options: allow `#`/`b` after a letter (the input then isn't strictly "one character per string", so the length cap counts notes, not characters), or a per-string picker instead of free text. Note `b` would conflict with forced uppercase unless flats are entered with `♭` or normalized to the sharp spelling the app already uses (`NOTES` is sharps-only).
  - **Editing a custom tuning.** E.g. long-press (or a pencil/edit mode, matching how hidden scales are edited) reopens the text field on that chip. Should editing it while it's the active tuning update the neck live as the user types, or only on confirm?
  - **Removing a custom tuning.** Probably the same edit mode with an ✕ per custom chip. If the active tuning is deleted, fall back to the instrument's first tuning (which is what the code already does for an unknown `tuningName`).
  - **Can the built-in tunings be edited or removed?** Simplest answer: no — they stay fixed so there's always a known-good tuning to return to, and custom tunings appear after them. If hiding built-ins is wanted, reuse the scale-list pattern (hide/restore in edit mode, never delete), and always keep at least one tuning visible.
  - **Banjo's short 5th string.** Banjo strings can be `{ note, startFret }` objects rather than plain letters. A typed banjo tuning would need to keep the drone string's start fret (5) and only change its note, so the text field sets pitches, not string layout.
  - **Naming.** Use the letters themselves as the chip label (`DADGAD`) unless naming becomes a need. Typing a tuning that matches an existing one (e.g. `DADGAD` on guitar, which is already built in) should select the existing chip instead of adding a duplicate.

- **First-run instrument picker (idea)**: instead of assuming mandolin for everyone (see **Mandolin as default instrument** above), show a one-time page right after the launch splash with big, thumb-sized buttons for each instrument (Guitar, Mandolin, Ukulele, Banjo). Tapping one saves it as the instrument and drops the user onto the fretboard; the instrument can still be changed later from the ••• sheet as today. Things to resolve before building:
  - **Detecting "first run."** Preferences are written to `fretwork.prefs.v1` as soon as the app mounts, so "no saved prefs" has to be checked before that first write (or tracked with an explicit flag such as `hasChosenInstrument`). Existing users, who already have saved prefs, should skip it.
  - **Skipping.** Whether there's a "skip" that falls back to mandolin, or the choice is required (it's a single tap either way).
  - **Visuals.** Text-only buttons vs. small instrument silhouettes; silhouettes read faster but are new art, and must work in both themes.
  - **Tuning.** Probably not asked here (the default tuning per instrument is fine; tuning stays in the ••• sheet), so the page stays a single question.
  - **Order with onboarding.** The "tap a fret to start a box" tooltip should appear after the picker, not on top of it.
  - **Instrument list growth.** Four buttons fit a phone screen comfortably; if 4-string banjos or other instruments are added, the page needs a layout that still fits without scrolling.

### Background: what does a double stop "mean" harmonically?

Worked example, G major, D + F#:

- D is scale degree 5, F# is degree 7; they're a major 3rd apart.
- A diatonic 3rd is two of the three notes of a triad, so on its own it's ambiguous — it always fits (at least) two chords: as **root + 3rd** of the chord built on the lower note, or as **3rd + 5th** of the chord a 3rd below that. D–F# is root+3rd of **D (V)** and 3rd+5th of **Bm (iii)**. What the ear actually hears depends on what the band/bass is playing underneath: over a D bass it's V, over G it's the top of a Gmaj7, over B it's Bm.
- **6ths are inverted 3rds.** F# below D (a minor 6th) is the same two notes, so it implies the same chords. Practical rule for labeling: for a 3rd, the "root+3rd" chord is named after the *lower* note; for a 6th, after the *upper* note.
- Useful property for bluegrass/I-IV-V music: the I, IV and V triads together contain all seven notes of the major scale, so nearly every diatonic 3rd sits inside one of them. In G major:

  | Dyad | root+3rd of | 3rd+5th of | Primary-chord reading |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | G–B | I (G) | vi (Em) | **I** |
  | A–C | ii (Am) | vii° (F#°) | ii — or the 5th+7th of **V7** (D7) |
  | B–D | iii (Bm) | I (G) | **I** |
  | C–E | IV (C) | ii (Am) | **IV** |
  | D–F# | V (D) | iii (Bm) | **V** |
  | E–G | vi (Em) | IV (C) | **IV** |
  | F#–A | vii° (F#°) | V (D) | **V** |

  So a labeling scheme that prefers I/IV/V when one applies gives a clear, practical label for 6 of 7 dyads, and "ii / V7" for the odd one out.
- **On G *minor***: F# isn't in G natural minor at all, so D–F# only shows up with harmonic minor — and there it's exactly the major V chord (D), which is the whole reason harmonic minor raises the 7th. Minor keys will need their own primary-chord preference (i, iv, V/v).
- Possible alternative framing worth comparing before building labels: invert it — the user picks the chord being played (I, IV, V…), and the neck highlights the double stops that belong to it. That answers "what can I play over this chord" directly instead of asking the player to decode a label per connector.
